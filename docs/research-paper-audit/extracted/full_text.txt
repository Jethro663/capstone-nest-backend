CHAPTER I 
INTRODUCTION
The landscape of modern education is undergoing a massive digital transformation by transitioning from traditional classroom environments to highly augmented digital ecosystems. Central to this evolution is the Learning Management System which has become a fundamental tool for educational institutions worldwide to facilitate lesson delivery, manage class rosters, and administer student assessments [1]. The basic architecture of a Learning Management System is heavily geared toward administrative control, structured content distribution, and rigorous compliance tracking [2]. In these environments, management and educators dictate exactly what content is delivered, the sequence of consumption, and the pacing of the overall course [3]. While the Learning Management System is exceptionally efficient at managing the logistical aspects of education and maintaining official academic records, the rigid top-down approach often fails to address the individualized cognitive needs of diverse student populations [4]. The global pandemic served as a catalyst for educational technology by forcing a rapid and often unplanned transition to remote and blended learning environments [1]. While this accelerated the adoption of basic Learning Management Systems including Moodle and Google Classroom to ensure instructional continuity and address skepticism toward online learning [2], it also exposed profound inequalities in how these systems handle diverse learning paces [3].
When institutions hastily implemented digital solutions, the focus was primarily on content delivery rather than cognitive scaffolding [1]. Consequently, students who lacked strong self-regulation skills or prerequisite knowledge found themselves rapidly falling behind and facing learning gaps that are exceptionally difficult to close once they occur [4]. The traditional Learning Management System is excellent at tracking completion but inherently lacks the diagnostic capability to identify the specific root causes of a student misunderstanding, which often renders standard online learning isolating for struggling learners [5].
For these students, the traditional Learning Management System often functions merely as a repository of static information rather than an active tool for academic recovery, leading to disengagement and compounded learning loss.
To address the inherent limitations of the traditional Learning Management System, the educational technology sector has seen the rise of the Learning Experience Platform. Unlike a Learning Management System, a Learning Experience Platform operates on a bottom-up learner centric paradigm that emphasizes extreme personalization, self-directed learning, and continuous skill growth [5]. A Learning Experience Platform utilizes advanced algorithms to curate diverse multi format digital resources including microlearning modules, podcasts, and interactive videos, tailoring the educational journey to a student specific goals and historical engagement patterns [2]. Research indicates that organizations and institutions deploying personalized experience driven platforms achieve thirty-five to fifty percent higher course completion rates and up to sixty percent greater learner engagement compared to traditional compliance focused Learning Management System environments [3]. Experimental studies further validate that targeted behavioral interventions through a Learning Experience Platform can extend daily average learning durations by forty-one-point five percent and significantly increase voluntary reading volumes, which positively correlates with overall academic performance [4].
The integration of Artificial Intelligence into these educational platforms represents a profound leap in capability, effectively bridging the gap between mass instruction and individualized support. Artificial Intelligence driven systems provide predictive analytics, enabling educators to anticipate academic risks early and deliver just in time support without disrupting core classroom instruction [1]. One of the most significant breakthroughs is the application of Large Language Models and Natural Language Processing to automate the generation of instructional materials. Traditional Natural Language Processing methods for extracting concepts from educational documents required resource intensive manual labor [3]. However, modern Large Language Models demonstrate advanced reasoning capabilities, utilizing techniques including Named Entity Recognition and Relation Extraction to automatically parse unstructured text including standard PDF modules into structured machine-readable instructional data [3].
 This allows the system to autonomously generate summaries, key points, quizzes, and personalized remedial paths, drastically reducing the instructional design burden on educators [4].
Beyond content generation, Artificial Intelligence serves as an active pedagogical agent through the deployment of the Intelligent Tutoring System. An Intelligent Tutoring System functions as a continuous twenty-four seven learning companion that adapts in real time to a student learning speed and specific knowledge gaps, providing immediate, judgment-free feedback [1]. Studies evaluating AI tutors in secondary education have demonstrated that students utilizing these systems achieve significantly higher learning gains in less time compared to traditional in-class active learning, with the median learning gains for AI-tutored students more than doubling those of control groups [2]. A highly nuanced subset of this technology is the integration of an anthropomorphic Non-Player Character (NPC) mentor. Engaging students emotionally and cognitively remains a pervasive challenge in secondary education, with interactive, persona-driven AI serving as a powerful mechanism to rebuild motivation [3]. Studies on the "Uncanny Valley" hypothesis in education reveal that a moderate degree of anthropomorphism in AI tutors enhances emotional engagement and generates high levels of learner trust, provided the AI is carefully designed to balance relatability with clear artificiality to avoid psychological discomfort [4].
The pedagogical logic for deploying these AI interventions relies heavily on Mastery Learning, an instructional philosophy positing that students must achieve a high level of competence in prerequisite knowledge before advancing [1]. Digital systems operationalize this through algorithms like Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT), which estimate a student's knowledge state and enforce mastery criteria [2]. While highly technical subjects may demand a 95 percent mastery threshold, practical applications in generalized secondary education frequently employ a  74% threshold to differentiate between basic comprehension and critical learning gaps [3]. Gating access to remedial interventions based on this threshold ensures that intensive, AI-driven support is aggressively targeted toward students who are fundamentally failing, preventing high-performing students from being subjected to redundant exercises [1].
In the Philippines, the Department of Education (DepEd) has actively pursued educational modernization through the Digital Rise Program, leading to the nationwide deployment of the DepEd Learning Management System (DLMS) [1]. Built to accommodate millions of learners, the DLMS serves as the primary repository for Electronic Self-Learning Modules (e-SLMs) and synchronous blended learning [2]. While the DLMS has successfully maintained instructional continuity and improved basic communication skills during crisis periods, [3] it remains a traditional, rigid LMS. It currently lacks the built-in, automated LXP features, personalized AI mentoring, and dynamic threshold-based remediation required to actively intervene when a student fails to comprehend an e-SLM [13].
Despite these technological advancements, a significant implementation gap currently exists in Philippine public secondary education. A comprehensive report by the ASEAN Foundation indicates that 83 percent of Filipino students actively utilize generative AI tools for educational purposes, yet only 42 percent of educators use these tools for their own tasks [1]. This profound generational and technological divide means that student AI usage is frequently driven by convenience rather than a strong technical understanding of responsible use [8]. Furthermore, students are integrating these technologies faster than curricula can adapt, leading to uncritical use and challenges for metacognitive engagement. [9] Fewer than half of educators express strong confidence in institutional AI policies, leaving schools without unified frameworks to harness AI safely and effectively [12].
 At Gat Andres Bonifacio High School, the challenges of delivering timely academic interventions are magnified by these technological limitations. Overcrowded classrooms and severe time constraints make the manual delivery of individualized remedial instruction nearly impossible [1]. While educators can identify struggling students through standard assessments, the absence of an integrated and automated intervention platform severely hampers consistent recovery efforts [3].
Therefore, the development of Nexora, which is a hybrid web and mobile platform combining robust Learning Management System administration with targeted Artificial Intelligence driven Learning Experience Platform remediation, is proposed to resolve this crisis. By automating module extraction and deploying an Artificial Intelligence Non-Player Character mentor strictly for students scoring below the mastery threshold, Nexora aims to systematically close competency gaps while profoundly reducing the unsustainable workload placed on local educators. By integrating these disparate technological advancements into a single architecture, the Nexora platform seeks to redefine the standard for digital remediation in the Philippines. The system prioritizes the automated extraction of core competencies from standard Department of Education materials, ensuring that the transition to a Learning Experience Platform is seamless and requires minimal manual input from teachers.
Through the deployment of an Artificial Intelligence Non-Player Character mentor, the platform provides a psychologically safe and engaging environment where students can master difficult concepts at their own pace [12]. Ultimately, the successful implementation of this hybrid model at Gat Andres Bonifacio High School could provide a scalable blueprint for other public institutions facing similar resource constraints and high student to teacher ratios.
1.1 PROJECT CONTEXT
The persistent challenge in modern secondary education is not merely the delivery of instruction but the equitable provision of timely and individualized remediation for students who fail to grasp core competencies. While Learning Management Systems have been widely adopted to streamline the distribution of curriculum and manage administrative workflows, a critical review of existing literature reveals a profound gap in their capacity to support personalized academic interventions [1]. Traditional Learning Management System platforms operate as static repositories because they excel at compliance tracking and grade logging but inherently lack the agile and adaptive mechanisms required to diagnose granular learning gaps and autonomously deploy customized recovery paths. Consequently, when a student struggles, the burden of intervention falls entirely on the classroom teacher who must manually analyze the data, curate alternative materials, and monitor the student progress offline. This process is highly unsustainable in public school environments where student-to-teacher ratios are high and resources are limited.
This technological gap is starkly evident within the Philippine K-12 educational landscape. In response to severe learning losses, DepEd introduced national remediation initiatives such as "Catch-Up Fridays," designed specifically to address foundational deficits in reading and mathematics [1]. However, empirical studies evaluating these programs highlight critical operational gaps at the local level. Researchers have documented that execution is severely hampered by insufficient targeted learning materials, inconsistent scheduling, and extreme educator fatigue [3]. Furthermore, a significant pedagogical gap exists regarding teacher specialization in remediation. Studies reveal that up to 75.3 percent of teachers assigned to handle remedial instruction are General Education specialists, with only 10.1 percent possessing specialized training in complex subjects like Mathematics [2]. Because effective remediation in technical subjects requires strong domain-specific knowledge to accurately diagnose and correct student misconceptions, placing this burden on unspecialized, overworked teachers severely degrades the quality of the intervention [14].
While the official DepEd Learning Management System (DLMS) provides excellent nationwide scalability for distributing Electronic Self-Learning Modules (e-SLMs), it fundamentally lacks the automated Learning Experience Platform (LXP) features necessary to support these struggling learners dynamically [1]. The DepEd Learning Management System (DLMS) does not possess the architecture to automatically trigger remedial pathways based on performance thresholds, nor does it integrate AI to adapt to individual student pacing [1]. Conversely, while isolated AI tutoring applications and commercial LXP systems excel at student engagement and personalized learning, [7] a structural gap exists as these platforms operate entirely outside the localized, highly structured curriculum and official grading frameworks required by Philippine public high schools. Additionally, despite 83 percent of Filipino students actively utilizing generative AI for schoolwork, there is a pronounced systemic gap in institutional readiness; schools lack the integrated platforms required to channel this AI usage into pedagogically sound, structured, and ethically monitored remedial environments [9].
Nexora is specifically engineered to bridge these critical gaps for Gat Andres Bonifacio High School. By fusing Learning Management System (LMS) and Learning Experience Platform (LXP) functionalities into a single, cohesive ecosystem, Nexora addresses the fragmentation between official curriculum management and self-directed intervention [6]. To solve the severe shortage of targeted learning materials and alleviate the workload on unspecialized teachers, Nexora leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to automatically extract core concepts, quizzes, and instructional formulas directly from uploaded, unstructured DepEd PDF modules [2]. This ensures that high-quality, relevant remedial content is always available without requiring manual authoring.
Furthermore, to address the pedagogical gap in delivering specialized support, Nexora deploys an anthropomorphic AI Non-Player Character (NPC) mentor [1]. This Intelligent Tutoring System (ITS) acts as a 24/7 tutor that explains mistakes, provides hints, and guides struggling students through complex topics, effectively compensating for the lack of domain-specific remedial teachers [2]. Crucially, to prevent the gamified Learning Experience Platform (LXP) environment from becoming a general distraction, Nexora introduces an automated, algorithmic triage system based on Mastery Learning principles [3]. The platform restricts Learning Experience Platform (LXP) access exclusively to students who score below a  74% performance threshold on standard Learning Management System (LMS) assessments [9].

1.2 PURPOSE AND DESCRIPTION
The primary purpose of this project is to design and develop Nexora, a highly integrated web and mobile-based Learning Management System featuring embedded Learning Experience Platform components, specifically engineered to automate and deliver targeted academic intervention for high school students at Gat Andres Bonifacio High School. Nexora aims to provide a comprehensive, unified digital ecosystem where educators can seamlessly manage official classes, lesson distributions, and standardized assessments, while simultaneously providing an autonomous, personalized remediation environment for students who demonstrate quantifiable learning difficulties.
What makes Nexora unique and highly relevant is its architectural solution to the critical gaps in public education: the seamless bridging of rigid curriculum compliance with agile, AI-driven intervention. The platform operates on a dynamic gating mechanism. Access to the highly engaging Learning Experience Platform (LXP) component is strictly limited to students who fail to achieve a defined mastery threshold (e.g., scoring below 74% on an assessment). This ensures that Learning Experience Platform (LXP) functions purely as a focused recovery tool rather than a general-purpose distraction. To resolve the massive burden of manual content creation on teachers, Nexora features automated instructional extraction. By leveraging advanced Large Language Models, the system digests upload unstructured learning modules (PDFs) and autonomously generates structured remedial formulas, quizzes, and summaries.
Furthermore, the system integrates an anthropomorphic Artificial Intelligence Non-Player Character (AI NPC) mentor within Learning Experience Platform (LXP). This intelligent tutor assists struggling learners by identifying their specific points of failure, explaining complex concepts in an adaptable, judgment-free manner, and recommending specific remedial exercises based on their performance data.
The target beneficiaries of this system are multi-fold. Students benefit directly from immediate, structured academic support tailored to their specific cognitive gaps, allowing them to rebuild confidence and achieve mastery without the stigma of falling behind their peers. Teachers benefit from intuitive, automated tools that handle the heavy lifting of diagnostic analysis and remedial content generation, drastically reducing their administrative burden and allowing them to focus on high-value, face-to-face instruction.






1.3 OBJECTIVES

	To design and develop Nexora, a web and mobile-based Learning Management System integrated with a Learning Experience Platform that supports all subjects and grade levels at Gat Andres Bonifacio High School, providing automated, targeted learning interventions for selected students, alleviating teacher workload through AI-driven content extraction.

To integrate an LXP within the LMS that acts as a targeted intervention environment, dynamically restricting access only to students demonstrating learning gaps by enforcing a strict 74% mastery threshold.

To evaluate the proposed system based on standard software metrics including functionality, reliability, usability, and portability.
To establish and maintain strict data security, ethical AI usage guidelines, and privacy protocols within the platform to address the lack of institutional AI frameworks and protect sensitive student academic records.





1.4 SCOPE AND DELIMITATION
SCOPE
The Nexora system is designed to support Grades 7, 8, 9, and 10 and subjects at Gat Andres Bonifacio High School through a responsive web and mobile-accessible application.
The system features core Learning Management System (LMS) functionalities, allowing teachers to create classes, upload lesson materials, administer assessments, and manage student rosters.
The platform includes a built-in Learning Experience Platform (LXP) designed exclusively for targeted academic intervention.
Access to the LXP environment is dynamically gated; it is accessible only to students who score below a defined 74% performance threshold on their LMS assessments.
The system incorporates Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Large Language Models (LLMs) to automatically extract text and generate structured instructional formulas, key points, and quizzes from uploaded PDF learning modules using Named Entity Recognition (NER) and Relation Extraction (RE).
An AI Non-Player Character (NPC) mentor (Intelligent Tutoring System) is integrated into the LXP to interact with struggling students, explain errors, provide hints, and guide them through remedial paths.
Teachers are provided with an administrative dashboard within the LMS to seamlessly monitor, track, and override LXP activities and student intervention progress.
A comprehensive system evaluation will be conducted assessing functionality, reliability, usability, and portability based on user feedback from selected students and faculty.
DELIMITATION
The system is designed to augment, not replace, traditional classroom instruction, teacher decision-making, or face-to-face pedagogy.
The platform excludes the full automation of official DepEd grading systems and does not replace the school's primary registrar or official master grading portal.
The LXP module functions strictly as a remedial intervention tool and is explicitly not intended for general, unrestricted use by high-performing students who surpass the mastery threshold.
Integration of remedial intervention scores back into the official LMS grade book is optional and intended primarily for teacher reference and formative assessment, rather than summative grading.
The system does not include advanced predictive analytics for predicting long-term high school dropout rates or large-scale AI model training from scratch (it utilizes existing Application Programming Interfaces (APIs)).
Deployment, testing, and implementation of the system beyond the specific localized context of Gat Andres Bonifacio High School are outside the scope of this study.
The system requires an active internet connection; offline functionality and synchronization are excluded.
Direct integration with third-party commercial content providers or external social media platforms is excluded.

2.1 RELATED LITERATURE/STUDIES
The transition from traditional, analog classroom instruction to digitally augmented pedagogical environments has necessitated a critical evaluation of how educational technology serves diverse learner profiles. The following subsections synthesize global and local studies to construct a comprehensive understanding of the technological mechanisms required to support struggling students without overburdening educators.
The Evolution and Paradigms of Learning Management Systems (LMS)
Historically, the Learning Management System (LMS) has functioned as the primary digital infrastructure for educational institutions. The fundamental architecture of a traditional Learning Management System (LMS) is designed around administrative control, hierarchical content distribution, and strict compliance tracking [1]. Within this framework, management and educational administrators determine precisely what content is delivered, the sequence in which it is consumed, and the pacing of the course [2].  Traditional Learning Management System (LMS) environments are characterized by catalogue management, centralized content storage, and monitoring of course completions against rigidly established learning outcomes [3]. While highly effective for standardized delivery and regulatory training including compliance courses or mandatory organizational onboarding, traditional Learning Management System environments are inherently rigid [1]. These systems dictate a top-down instructional path where learning is mandatory and structurally inflexible [2]. Research indicates that while Learning Management System platforms excel in formal training and generating reports on completion rates, scores, and the duration of content engagement, they frequently fail to generate intrinsic learner motivation or adapt to individual cognitive deficits [3].
For struggling students, the Learning Management System often becomes a repository of frustration. When a student fails to grasp a concept within the structured Learning Management System, the system typically offers no alternative but to force the student to repeat the exact same linear module, which often exacerbates disengagement and cognitive overload [5].
Learning Experience Platforms (LXP): Learner-Centric Paradigms and Engagement
In response to the limitations of traditional Learning Management System (LMS) architectures, the Learning Experience Platform (LXP) emerged as a paradigm shift toward learner-centric, self-directed education. Unlike the closed syllabus approach of a Learning Management System (LMS), a Learning Experience Platform (LXP) operates on principles of extreme personalization, utilizing artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms to curate and recommend multi-format resources tailored to a learner's specific goals, skill gaps, and historical engagement patterns [4]. The pedagogical philosophy underpinning the Learning Experience Platform (LXP) is the facilitation of autonomous learning. Learning Experience Platform (LXP) fosters this autonomy by incorporating microlearning, social collaboration, and gamification, thereby shifting the locus of control from the system administrator directly to the learner [1]. By analyzing data on learners' interactions, Learning Experience Platform (LXP) provides a flexible approach to education that mimics popular content consumption algorithms, actively suggesting relevant videos, podcasts, articles, and interactive modules [9].
The empirical impacts of Learning Experience Platform (LXP)s on learner engagement and performance are substantial. Research comparing the two systems indicates that Learning Experience Platform (LXP)s generate significantly higher intrinsic motivation. Organizations deploying personalized, experience-driven platforms report 35 to 50 percent higher course completion rates and up to 74% greater learner engagement compared to traditional, compliance-focused Learning Management System (LMS) environments [1]. Furthermore, experimental studies examining the impact of personalized learning experience platforms on student performance demonstrate that targeted behavioral interventions through a Learning Experience Platform (LXP) can extend daily average learning durations by 41.5 percent and increase voluntary literature reading volumes by 48.3 percent [2]. Correlation analyses in these studies show that the duration of self-directed learning within a Learning Experience Platform (LXP) has a significant positive correlation with overall academic performance, validating the platform's mechanism for promoting deep, sustained learning [11].

In the context of formal K-12 and secondary education, the complete abandonment of the Learning Management System (LMS) in favor of a Learning Experience Platform (LXP) is pedagogically and administratively unsound. Public school systems require rigorous adherence to standardized curricula, official grading frameworks, and structured lesson delivery that only a Learning Management System (LMS) can reliably provide [1]. This transition reframes the recovery process, allowing the system to deploy adaptive difficulty, diverse media formats, and personalized pacing to close the specific competency gap without disrupting the progression of the broader class [2]. Integrating these platforms ensures equitable access to adaptive tools, promoting self-directed learning interventions that maintain instructional structure while fostering autonomy [3].
Artificial Intelligence in Education: From Automation to Adaptive Interventions
The integration of Artificial Intelligence into educational technology represents a profound leap in the capabilities of learning management systems. For decades, adaptive education was bottlenecked by the impossibility of manually creating enough bespoke content to cater to every individual student learning profile. Artificial Intelligence fundamentally resolves this scalability issue by providing real time data analysis, predictive analytics, and automated content generation [12].
A recent systematic review of twenty-seven peer reviewed studies analyzed the integration of Artificial Intelligence powered Application Programming Interfaces in education [2]. Predictive analytics allow educational platforms to anticipate academic risks early, enabling educators to provide just in time support across subjects without disrupting core instruction [1]. Artificial Intelligence driven adaptive interventions continuously monitor student interactions, identifying granular competency gaps through performance analysis. Once a gap is detected, Artificial Intelligence delivers tailored content specifically designed to address the misunderstanding [2]. Studies report that the implementation of Artificial Intelligence driven adaptive feedback systems yields significant improvements in student comprehension and academic achievement [3]. However, the rapid adoption of Artificial Intelligence also raises critical challenges. While students are embracing generative Artificial Intelligence at unprecedented rates, institutional readiness lags significantly [1]. A large-scale national survey involving over fifteen thousand students revealed an implementation gap where student practices are evolving faster than pedagogical frameworks, leading to uncritical use and challenges in metacognitive engagement [2]. Furthermore, integrating Artificial Intelligence into Kindergarten to Grade 12 environments requires navigating complex privacy regulations and ensuring that Artificial Intelligence tools augment rather than replace teacher capabilities [3]. Effective Artificial Intelligence implementation must focus on handling routine tasks, such as grading and content recommendation, thereby freeing educators to focus on high value instructional mentoring and relationship building [5].
Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Large Language Models (LLMs) for Automated Content Extraction
A persistent challenge in operating adaptive learning environments is the sheer volume of structured instructional data required. Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI), specifically Large Language Models (LLMs), addresses this by automating the creation and extraction of learning materials from unstructured sources [1]. Large Language Models (LLMs) perform self-supervised learning on large-scale corpora to obtain the statistical laws of language, enabling them to comprehend natural language, extract complex information, and generate high-quality text content [2]. Recent computational research emphasizes the efficacy of utilizing Large Language Models (LLMs) to parse unstructured educational documents, such as PDF modules and presentation slides, to generate structured instructional data [1]. Traditional Natural Language Processing (NLP) methods for concept extraction required resource-intensive manual annotation and precise dataset preparation, which failed to minimize labor for educators [5].
Modern Large Language Models (LLMs), however, demonstrate advanced zero-shot and few-shot reasoning capabilities to automatically identify key concepts, generate summaries, and map competency relationships directly from course descriptions and syllabi [1]. The automated extraction process typically involves complex document chunking methodologies. To navigate token limits and prevent the model from losing critical context, documents are segmented into optimized chunks (e.g., 1000-token blocks) [9]. Following chunking, systems employ multi-step prompting techniques for Named Entity Recognition (NER) to isolate instructional variables, key terms, and learning objectives [2].
Finally, relation extraction (RE) prompts retrieve key details and structure the outputs into machine-readable formats, such as JSON schemas, for downstream integration into the Learning Management System (LMS) database [3]. Empirical evaluations of this multi-step LLM extraction process show that models like GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o achieve remarkably high accuracy, precision, and F1 scores in educational content scenarios, outperforming traditional approaches [3]. In the context of instructional design, AI-driven Automated Learning Management Systems (autoLMS) utilize these extraction protocols to digest uploaded course materials and autonomously output customized resources, including summaries, key points, lecture notes, quizzes, and automated instructional formulas [1]. This capability is critical for secondary education ecosystems where teachers manage large class sizes and lack the temporal bandwidth to manually design individualized remedial paths [13]. By automating the extraction of core competencies from standard learning modules, Artificial Intelligence (AI) empowers the Learning Management System (LMS) to dynamically assemble targeted micro-lessons that directly address a student's demonstrated points of failure, effectively bridging the gap between static curriculum and adaptive remediation [7].
Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) and Anthropomorphic AI Mentors
Beyond automated content generation, Artificial Intelligence serves as an active agent in the learning process through Intelligent Tutoring Systems and adaptive feedback mechanisms. Artificial Intelligence tutors function as continuous, twenty-four seven learning companions, utilizing machine learning and natural language processing to track hundreds of data points per session [1]. These systems adapt in real time to a student learning speed, presentation preferences, and knowledge gaps [2]. For example, an Artificial Intelligence tutor might recognize that a student struggles with abstract algebraic equations and dynamically shift to providing visual models or step by step simulations tailored to the student personal interests [9].
Empirical evidence strongly supports the efficacy of Artificial Intelligence tutors in secondary education. Studies demonstrate that students utilizing Artificial Intelligence tutors achieve significantly higher learning gains compared to those receiving traditional in class active learning [1]. In rigorous quantitative assessments, the median learning gains for students in Artificial Intelligence tutored groups were over double those of control groups, with statistical analyses revealing highly significant differences [14]. Furthermore, students report feeling significantly more engaged and motivated during Artificial Intelligence assisted sessions [2]. The efficacy of Artificial Intelligence tutors is particularly pronounced among students with lower baseline knowledge and stronger self-regulation skills, suggesting that Artificial Intelligence integration thrives when students can strategically combine independent study with targeted, machine guided support [3].
A highly nuanced subset of Artificial Intelligence tutoring involves the deployment of anthropomorphic non-Player Character mentors. As student engagement remains a pervasive challenge, with studies indicating up to 72 percent of secondary students exhibiting low behavioral engagement and 70 percent exhibiting low cognitive engagement, interactive, persona driven Artificial Intelligence serves as a powerful mechanism to rebuild emotional investment [10]. However, the design and implementation of Artificial Intelligence mentors are subject to the psychological phenomenon known as the Uncanny Valley hypothesis [2].
A comprehensive mixed methods study investigating the impact of anthropomorphic features in Artificial Intelligence tutoring systems on learner trust utilized an eight-item questionnaire and exploratory factor analysis to identify two key dimensions: competence and trustworthiness and emotional engagement [1]. The results confirmed an inverted U relationship between anthropomorphism and student trust. A moderate degree of human like relatability enhances emotional engagement and generates the highest levels of trust, but excessive realism induces psychological discomfort, skepticism, and a reduction in learning motivation [7]. The study also identified a gender effect, where female students reported greater trust in high anthropomorphism conditions compared to male students [1]. Furthermore, student interactions with Artificial Intelligence mentors are heavily influenced by pre-existing technological biases. A binary logistic and multiple linear regression analysis of college students evaluating Artificial Intelligence generated mentorship revealed that prior exposure to tools like ChatGPT predicted greater accuracy in identifying Artificial Intelligence responses and more favorable ratings of the Artificial Intelligence helpfulness [1].
Conversely, students who viewed human mentorship as exclusively effective were less likely to seek support from Artificial Intelligence perceived responses, indicating that pre-existing biases shape engagement more than the actual content provided by the Artificial Intelligence [1]. Therefore, an effective Artificial Intelligence Non-Player Character mentor must be carefully engineered to balance pedagogical authority with an approachable, clearly artificial persona to maximize targeted intervention outcomes without causing psychological dissonance [8].
Mastery Learning Frameworks and Performance Thresholds in Remedial Education
The pedagogical foundation for gating access to remedial Learning Experience Platform environments within a blended system relies entirely on the principles of Mastery Learning. Originally introduced in the 1960s by educational psychologists, mastery learning posits that students must achieve a predefined, high level of competence in prerequisite knowledge before they are permitted to advance to subsequent, more complex material [1]. Unlike traditional, time-based educational models that push entire cohorts forward regardless of individual comprehension, mastery models utilize continuous formative assessments to identify specific competency levels [2]. If a student fails to meet the required threshold, they do not advance. Instead, they are diverted into corrective activities, peer tutoring, small group discussions, or specialized digital remediation until mastery is unequivocally demonstrated [3].
Mastery learning has been empirically proven to be a highly powerful instructional method, yielding moderate to large effect sizes ranging from 0.50 to 0.58 across all levels of education [1]. Determining the precise threshold for mastery, however, is a subject of extensive educational data mining research. Systems employing Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, which is a standard algorithm used in intelligent tutoring systems to model student knowledge, frequently analyze student performance to set binary indicators of mastery [2].
While some highly rigorous technical subjects demand a 90 to 95 percent mastery criterion, and recent Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) research suggests that pushing thresholds to 0.98 can yield additional benefits for future learning retention, [11] practical implementations in generalized secondary education frequently utilize a 74 percent threshold to differentiate between basic comprehension and critical learning gaps. [4] Research highlights that in large public education systems, particularly in the United States and developing nations, approximately 40 to 60 percent of first-year college students require some form of remediation in core subjects like mathematics and reading [3].
 In secondary environments, establishing a 74 percent performance threshold ensures that intervention resources are aggressively targeted toward students who are fundamentally failing to grasp the material, rather than forcing high-performing students through redundant, demotivating exercises [1]. This threshold acts as an automated triage mechanism. By configuring the Learning Management System (LMS) to monitor assessment scores, the system can seamlessly shift students scoring below the 74% metric into specialized, AI-guided Learning Experience Platform (LXP) remediation environments. This ensures that the time-intensive process of relearning foundational concepts is restricted to those who genuinely require it, thereby optimizing instructional efficiency and preventing long-term academic stagnation [2].
The Philippine Educational Technology Landscape: Initiatives and Systemic Challenges
The integration of advanced Learning Management System and Artificial Intelligence features must be critically contextualized within the unique socio economic and infrastructural challenges of the Philippine educational system. Over recent years, the Department of Education has actively pursued technological modernization, fundamentally accelerated by the disruptions of the global pandemic [1]. Central to this modernization is the Digital Rise Program, an educational framework designed to anchor the infrastructure, software, and capacity building of both learners and teachers in technology [2]. The Digital Rise Program comprises three core components. The first is Digital Literacy, which updates the Kindergarten to Grade 12 curriculum to include productivity tools, basic programming, and multimedia design. The second is ICT Assisted Teaching, which aims to provide educators with necessary equipment like laptops and smart TVs. The third is ICT Assisted Learning for Learners, which grants student access to synchronous blended learning [12]. A cornerstone of this final component is the Department of Education Learning Management System [1]. Built upon an open-source Moodle framework, this system is engineered for national scalability [1]. It serves as the primary platform for conducting online classes, administering national tests, and distributing Electronic Self Learning Modules, alongside DepEd TV and Radio episodes [2].
Local empirical studies confirm that the utilization of Learning Management System platforms during and after the pandemic significantly improved academic performance, maintained instructional continuity, and fostered a favorable perception of distance learning among Philippine secondary school students [3]. Researchers emphasize that Learning Management System utilization positively affected communication skills and critical thinking, with recommendations strongly urging the institutionalization of the Learning Management System in public secondary schools [4].
Despite these structural advancements, there is a pronounced disparity in Artificial Intelligence (AI) readiness and digital literacy between Philippine students and educators. A comprehensive 2026 report by the ASEAN Foundation indicated that the Philippines ranks among the early adopters of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Southeast Asia, with 83 percent of Filipino students utilizing generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) models (such as ChatGPT) for educational purposes, primarily for writing tasks and information retrieval [1]. In stark contrast, only 42 percent of teachers use Artificial Intelligence (AI) for their own tasks, and only 73 percent of educators utilize generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) models for teaching [9]. This generational gap in technology adoption means that high student usage is frequently driven by convenience rather than a strong technical understanding of responsible Artificial Intelligence (AI) use [11]. Furthermore, the report found that fewer than half of educators express strong confidence in institutional AI policies, and only half of the schools provide adequate training or clear usage guidelines [15]. The lack of a unified national framework for Artificial Intelligence (AI) in basic education has left teachers struggling to adapt their instructional strategies to a rapidly changing digital landscape [2].
Remedial Instruction Difficulties in Philippine Public Secondary Schools
The technological disparities mentioned above heavily compound the pedagogical challenges of providing effective remedial instruction in Philippine public high schools. Institutions like Gat Andres Bonifacio High School frequently contend with overcrowded classrooms, severe teacher workloads, and a lack of specialized learning resources [1].
 In response to pandemic-related learning loss, DepEd introduced "Catch-Up Fridays," a nationwide initiative dedicated to addressing academic gaps in reading and mathematics [2]. However, studies evaluating the implementation of such remedial programs reveal profound operational difficulties. An investigation into Catch-Up Fridays at a public senior high school in Los Baños, utilizing surveys and semi-structured interviews with 121 students and 15 teachers, uncovered that while the program's objectives are sound, execution is severely hampered. Students reported insufficient learning materials, inconsistent scheduling, and restricted access to technology, while teachers highlighted heavy workloads, lack of training, and unclear policy directives [1]. Similarly, a study at Bolo Norte High School on remedial reading for Grade 7 students found that while intervention significantly improved reading comprehension (shifting scores from "Emerging" to "Developing," p < 0.000), the post-test scores still fell short of intended outcomes, necessitating continuous, structural extension services [8].
Furthermore, researchers have identified a critical mismatch in teacher specializations concerning remedial instruction. A 2024 study analyzing the demographic profiles of teachers providing remediation revealed that 75.3 percent were General Education specialists, while only 10.1 percent possessed specialized training in Mathematics [1]. Given that subjects like mathematics require strong content knowledge and effective pedagogical strategies to diagnose specific student misconceptions, assigning unspecialized teachers to remediation efforts severely degrades the quality of interventions [7]. In rural and underserved areas, where professional development opportunities are scarce, this lack of domain-specific expertise makes the manual diagnosis of learning gaps and the design of individualized remedial paths nearly impossible [15].
2.2 RELATED SYSTEMS
2.2.1 Foreign Systems
Cypher Learning Learning Management System (LMS)
Cypher Learning is a prominent cloud-based platform that heavily integrates artificial intelligence to bridge the functional gap between a traditional Learning Management System (LMS) and a modern Learning Experience Platform (LXP) [1]. Its standout feature is Artificial Intelligence (AI) 360, a suite powered by Microsoft Copilot that allows educators to automatically generate course content, gamification elements, and competency mappings from existing PDFs and videos [2]. Crucially, Cypher Learning employs robust adaptive learning mechanics utilizing threshold rules [3]. Educators can set minimum achievement thresholds; if a student's performance falls below a defined parameter (e.g., 50 percent), the system's smart recommendation engine automatically triggers targeted interventions. These automated actions include hiding advanced content, revealing simpler remedial modules, sending personalized notifications, or enrolling the student in specific micro-classes without requiring manual teacher input [3]. Cypher Learning validates the technical feasibility of automating remedial pathways based on specific performance thresholds and utilizing Artificial Intelligence (AI) for content extraction.
SchoolAI
SchoolAI represents a highly targeted approach to integrating generative Artificial Intelligence directly into student interactions, focusing almost exclusively on personalized tutoring rather than broad administrative management. The platform centers around Artificial Intelligence tutors referred to as Spaces which adapt in real time to a student learning pace, preferences, and knowledge gaps [1]. The Artificial Intelligence acts as a twenty-four seven learning companion that provides immediate feedback, explains complex concepts using interactive visual models, and maintains a judgment free environment for struggling learners [2]. Furthermore, SchoolAI provides educators with real time dashboards detailing exactly which concepts are challenging individual students, drastically reducing administrative oversight and allowing teachers to step in only for critical moments of instruction [4].
Lummio
Lummio is a comprehensive learning management system specifically designed to track district-wide student outcomes, manage coursework, and streamline administrative workflows [1]. Its primary strength lies in its ability to aggregate data across multiple educational locations, monitor real-time attendance, and track broad academic progress to identify systemic learning gaps [1]. By centralizing communication, interactive assessments, and grading tools into a single platform, Lummio significantly reduces the administrative burden on educators, allowing program coordinators to focus on direct student support [2]. Lummio’s robust data tracking and progress monitoring features serve as an excellent model for the administrative and teacher-facing oversight interfaces required in a high school Learning Management System (LMS).
ALEKS (Assessment and Learning in Knowledge Spaces)
While primarily a mathematical tutoring system rather than a general-purpose Learning Management System (LMS), ALEKS exemplifies the rigorous application of mastery thresholds and artificial intelligence. It utilizes Artificial Intelligence (AI) to map a student's precise knowledge state and strictly gates progression based on demonstrated competence [10]. Research on systems like ALEKS proves that dynamically adjusting mastery thresholds based on content difficulty and student retention rates significantly improves learning efficiency [7]. Its algorithmic approach to identifying the exact prerequisites a student is lacking informs the logic required for routing students to targeted remedial content.
2.2.2 Local Systems
DepEd Learning Management System (DLMS)
The DepEd Learning Management System (DLMS) is the official, localized system utilized by the Philippine Department of Education. Built on an open-source Moodle framework, the DepEd Learning Management System (DLMS) is engineered for massive scalability to accommodate millions of K-12 learners nationwide [14]. It primarily functions as a repository and delivery mechanism for Electronic Self-Learning Modules (e-SLMs), DepEd TV, and DepEd Radio episodes [2]. While highly effective for centralized content distribution and asynchronous blended learning during crisis periods, the DepEd Learning Management System (DLMS) is fundamentally a traditional, rigid Learning Management System (LMS). It lacks built-in, automated Learning Experience Platform (LXP) features, personalized Artificial Intelligence (AI) mentoring, or dynamic threshold-based remediation [12]. Consequently, when a student struggles with an e-SLM within the DepEd Learning Management System (DLMS), teachers must manually intervene, review the data, and design a custom remedial path offline.


Vidyalaya Learning Management System (LMS) and Rex Education Schoology
Private sector adaptations in the Philippines, such as Rex Education’s deployment of Schoology and Vidyalaya Learning Management System (LMS), provide customizable, performance-focused learning solutions [1]. These platforms bridge traditional Learning Management System (LMS) functionality with modern learning experiences through advanced reporting and learner-centric features. However, their primary market focus leans heavily toward institutional compliance, continuous corporate learning, and generalized distance education rather than targeted, automated high school academic remediation utilizing generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) [11]. While successful in combining structured paths with digital engagement, they do not offer the specific Artificial Intelligence (AI) NPC mentorship or automated module extraction required to alleviate public school teacher workloads.

2.3 SYNTHESIS
The review of literature and related systems underscores a critical paradigm shift in educational technology: the necessary transition from passive content management systems to active, personalized learning intervention platforms. While traditional Learning Management Systems (LMS) have successfully digitized administrative tasks, course scheduling, and formal content delivery, they inherently fail to address the individualized cognitive needs of struggling learners. To support these students using a traditional Learning Management System (LMS) requires, unsustainable manual input from educators who must diagnose gaps, create alternative materials, and monitor individual progress offline. Conversely, isolated Learning Experience Platforms (LXPs) and intelligent Artificial Intelligence (AI) tutoring applications provide excellent engagement, adaptive pacing, and personalization, but they often operate completely outside the rigorous, structured curriculum management and grading frameworks required by formal public educational institutions. The primary justification for the present study lies in the unaddressed technological intersection of these platforms, particularly within the localized, highly constrained context of Philippine public secondary education.
At Gat Andres Bonifacio High School, educators manage exceptionally large class sizes and face severe instructional time constraints, making the manual delivery of individualized remedial instruction nearly impossible. National educational initiatives, such as DepEd’s Catch-Up Fridays, highlight a desperate, systemic need for remediation in core competencies like reading and mathematics. However, current literature explicitly proves that these efforts are bottlenecked by a lack of specialized teachers, insufficient targeted learning materials, and extreme educator fatigue. Furthermore, while the existing DepEd DLMS is highly scalable and effective for module distribution, it simply does not possess the technological architecture to autonomously support these remedial initiatives or harness the generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools that students are already using independently.
The proposed system, Nexora, is designed specifically to eliminate this gap by engineering a unified, hybrid Learning Management System (LMS)- Learning Experience Platform (LXP) environment tailored for the public high school ecosystem. This project is not a duplication of existing commercial software; it is a highly specific, context-aware architectural solution. By enforcing an automated 74% performance threshold on standard assessments within the Learning Management System (LMS), Nexora utilizes data-driven triage to ensure that the gamified Learning Experience Platform (LXP) environment functions strictly as a targeted intervention tool. This prevents the Learning Experience Platform (LXP) from becoming a general-purpose distraction and ensures that remediation resources are directed exclusively at students who demonstrate quantifiable learning gaps. This algorithmic routing is firmly supported by the established pedagogical principles of Mastery Learning and Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, which validate that immediate, targeted remediation prevents compounding academic failure.
Furthermore, Nexora resolves the material shortage and teacher workload dilemma through the integration of Large Language Model (LLM) automated content extraction. By allowing the system to ingest standard, unstructured DepEd PDF modules and autonomously extract key concepts, instructional formulas, and remedial exercises, the platform entirely removes the burden of manual content creation from the general education teacher who may lack domain-specific expertise.

FUNCTIONAL DECOMPOSITION DIAGRAM
The Functional Decomposition Diagram (FDD) represents a hierarchical breakdown of the Nexora system into its core functional modules and sub-features. This structure ensures that complex administrative and pedagogical objectives are divided into manageable components for systematic development [9].

Figure 5: Functional Decomposition Diagram of the Admin Portal
The Admin Portal FDD illustrates the system-wide oversight and health monitoring modules required for school management. It decomposes the portal into critical modules including Admin Dashboard (for total user and active class snapshots), System Diagnostics (for monitoring AI services and database pools), and User Management (for role-based account directories). Additionally, it includes Academic Logistics and System Configuration to ensure the platform stays synchronized with the physical school calendar and section rosters.

Figure 6: Functional Decomposition Diagram of the Teacher Portal
 The Teacher Portal FDD presents the instructional management framework, emphasizing the "Teacher as Validator" role. It breaks down the system into the My Classes Hub for primary lesson delivery and the AI Support Workspace, which facilitates automated PDF content extraction and AI-assisted quiz drafting. The diagram also details the Intervention Module, which prioritizes struggling students and tracks the final outcomes of AI-led remedial sessions.


Figure 7: Functional Decomposition Diagram of the Student Portal
 The Student Portal FDD outlines the personalized learning journey and the mastery-gated recovery path. The hierarchy is organized around the Learning Dashboard for pending tasks and the LXP Remedial Workspace, which provides the student with adaptive playlists and access to the AI Tutoring (JAKIPIR) system. It also includes Performance Tracking to provide students with metacognitive insights into their quarterly academic trends and assessment history.
SYSTEM FLOWCHART/FRAMEWORK
The system flowchart presents the overall workflow of the Nexora Hybrid Educational Ecosystem, showing the sequence of processes including secure role-based authentication, automated AI content extraction from Department of Education (DepEd) PDF modules, mastery-gated summative assessments, and personalized remedial interventions. It illustrates how administrators, teachers, and students interact with the unified platform and how academic data flows across the NestJS backend core and the FastAPI AI microservice, ensuring that pedagogical pathways are logically connected and aligned with Mastery Learning principles. This visual representation helps simplify complex event-driven workflows and supports better system analysis for academic recovery at Gat Andres Bonifacio High School.





















Mobile Application Flows (Student)
Figure 8: Mobile Login & OTP Verification
	Figure 8 illustrates the process by which a student accesses the Nexora mobile application using their registered credentials. It begins when the student opens the application and inputs their email and password, followed by system-performed credential validation. If it is the user’s first login, the system triggers a multi-factor verification flow requiring a One-Time Password (OTP) and a mandatory password update; otherwise, the user is directly authenticated and redirected to the dashboard. This diagram clearly shows the interaction between the student and the system through defined actions and decision points, ensuring a secure and structured entry into the ecosystem.
















Figure 9: Process Flow of Mobile Forgot Password and Account Recovery
Figure 9 represents the account recovery process for students who have lost access to their credentials. It begins when the student selects the "Forgot Password" option and enters their registered email address, prompting the system to validate the record in the database. If the email exists, the system generates and sends a secure reset link to the user; otherwise, an error message is displayed for retry. This process ensures a structured and secure method for updating credentials and returning the student to the login interface.
Figure 10: Process Flow of View Dashboard and "Continue Learning" Logic
Figure 10 highlights the intelligent navigation within the student's mobile dashboard designed to facilitate instructional continuity. Upon successful authentication, the system fetches the student’s progress data to identify the last lesson or assessment accessed and renders a dynamic "Continue Learning" card. The student can click this card to be immediately redirected to their current point in the curriculum, ensuring a seamless transition between study sessions. This diagram visualizes how the system leverages historical engagement patterns to streamline the learner's journey.

Figure 11: Process Flow of View Modules and Time-Based Engagement Tracking
Figure 11 illustrates the interaction between the student and the learning modules; The system tracks the duration of assessment attempts in the database to monitor how long a student interacts with a module. This ensures that engagement is quantified before a student proceeds to assessments, rather than simply marking a module as complete upon opening.
Figure 12: Process Flow of Taking Assessments and Summative Submission
Figure 12 details the workflow for completing and submitting academic assessments through the mobile interface. It begins when the student selects an assignment and fetches the corresponding question set, followed by the input of answers or file uploads. Upon submission, the system performs an immediate calculation of the raw score and records the attempt in the PostgreSQL database. This diagram shows the direct feedback loop between the student's submission and the generation of their performance summary.

Figure 13: Process Flow of LXP Remedial Access and 74% Mastery Threshold Gating
Figure 13 visualizes the critical "logical spine" of the Nexora system: the automated triage of struggling students. After an assessment is submitted, the system compares the calculated score against a defined 74% mastery threshold. If the score falls below this floor, the system triggers an "At-Risk" state, unlocks the Learning Experience Platform (LXP) sidebar, and notifies the teacher; if the threshold is met, the student continues the standard linear path. This diagram highlights the dynamic state changes that govern access to remedial resources.

Figure 14: Process Flow of RAG-Based Interaction with JAKIPIR AI Mentor
Figure 14 illustrates the sophisticated retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline used during student interactions with the JAKIPIR AI Mentor. When a student inputs a natural language query regarding a concept failure, the FastAPI microservice performs a vector similarity search in the pgvector database to retrieve relevant chunks of the original DepEd PDF. The system then synthesizes an adaptive hint that provides instructional guidance without giving the direct answer. This flow ensures that AI support is strictly grounded in authorized class materials to prevent hallucinations and maintain academic integrity.
Figure 15: Process Flow of View Performance Analytics and Quarterly Trends
Figure 15 represents the process by which students monitor their own academic growth through the performance tab. The system aggregates all recorded assessment scores and computes a blended average to render visual quarterly trend charts and identify specific learning gaps. This diagram shows how the system transforms raw data into metacognitive insights, allowing students to visualize which competencies require additional focus through the LXP.

Figure 16: Process Flow of Mobile Profile Management and Data Persistence
Figure 16 details the profile management workflow on the mobile side, emphasizing the lack of detail-locking as identified in QA testing. It begins when the student accesses their profile to modify personal information or upload an avatar, followed by a system validation of the mobile number format. Once saved, the updated information is synchronized with the NestJS backend and reflected across the web portal. This diagram visualizes the data persistence loop between the mobile client and the centralized database.
